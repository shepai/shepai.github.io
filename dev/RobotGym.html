Overview

The aim is teleoperation without a controller. A person moves, a camera-based body tracker turns their movement into the positions of their hands and feet (and, where it can, elbows, knees, hips and shoulders), and a humanoid robot copies them without falling over. This page covers the first half: the simulated robot and how it is trained to copy poses. The second half, connecting a real-time pose tracker, is still being built.

All of the learning happens in simulation, because a robot learning to balance falls over thousands of times. We use reinforcement learning: a small neural network, the policy, looks at the robot 50 times a second and decides where every joint should move next. After each step a reward scores how well that went, and the learning algorithm, PPO [1], nudges the network towards whatever earned more reward.

The humanoid

The robot is the Unitree G1, simulated in MuJoCo [2] from Unitree’s 29-joint model file, g1_29dof.xml [3]. The same proportions are used by OpenG1, an open-source 3D-printable humanoid, so what is learned here is meant to carry over to it.

How the robot is controlled

The policy doesn’t command torques directly. Every 20 ms it outputs a target angle for each joint, and a PD controller running at 500 Hz turns the gap between target and actual angle into a motor torque. The controller uses the stiffness from Unitree’s own G1 configuration [3]: 100 to 150 N·m per radian at the hips and knees, 40 at the ankles. The wrists are held still for now, so the policy drives 23 of the 29 joints.

Training process

First attempt: borrowed legs

Unitree publishes a pretrained walking policy for the G1 [3]. The first plan was to let it run the legs, holding the robot still, and train a second policy for the waist and arms to reach hand targets. It never learned: across a 1.5 million step run, episodes ended after about 45 of their 500 steps, so the robot fell within a second every time.

The cause wasn’t the learning. Running the walking policy on its own showed it couldn’t keep the 29-joint robot standing either:

SetupStays up for
12-joint G1 model (what the walking policy was trained on)10 s (full episode)
29-joint model, arms perfectly still1.6 s
29-joint model, arms exploring randomly0.4 to 1.3 s
29-joint model with joint armature restored10 s, even with random arm motion

Unitree’s 29-joint model sets no armature on any joint, while the 12-joint model the walking policy learned on uses 0.01 kg·m². Armature is the motor’s rotor inertia as felt at the joint, the rotor inertia multiplied by the gear ratio squared [4]. It is small, but a gearbox makes it matter, and a controller tuned with it behaves very differently without it. Putting it back fixed standing outright. The environment now uses per-motor values from BeyondMimic’s G1 configuration [5].

Lesson: a reward can’t fix a simulation that is wrong about the hardware. Every motor property and link mass in the model has to match the real robot before a policy trained on it can work there.

One policy for the whole body

A walking policy only knows how to step; it can’t hold a foot where you ask. So we replaced it with a single policy that drives all 23 joints, following the motion-imitation approach of DeepMimic [6] and H2O [7].

  • Targets: 12 keypoints (hands, feet, elbows, knees, shoulders and hips), measured relative to the robot’s pelvis and the direction it faces, so the same numbers can later come straight from a body tracker.
  • What the policy sees: the IMU (rotation rate and tilt), joint angles and speeds, its last action, the target keypoints, how far its own keypoints are from them, and the reference joint angles.
  • What it outputs: a small correction added to the reference joint angles. On top of that, a gravity feed-forward term adds the torque needed to hold the reference pose up. Without it, the untrained robot’s pose error after 0.2 s is 4.7 cm; with it, 1.4 cm.
  • Episodes: each one starts at a random moment of a random reference clip, with the robot already in that pose. It ends early if the robot falls (pelvis tilted past 45° or 25 cm too low) or drifts more than 30 cm from the pose on average.

Reference motions

The policy needs lots of poses that are physically possible. A generator builds them with whole-body inverse kinematics, keeping the centre of mass over the feet on the ground, checking that limbs don’t collide and limiting joint speed to 3 rad/s. The library has 120 clips, 43 minutes in all, in four kinds:

KindWhat happensFeet
upperArm and waist movementsboth planted
wholeSquats, leans, twists and weight shifts, with arm movementsboth planted
balanceKnee raises and foot reaches on one legone lifted, then put back
stepSteps to new spots and turnsone lifted, then placed somewhere new

Recorded human movement can be added too. A retargeting step solves inverse kinematics to turn tracker keypoints into a full robot pose in about 2 ms per frame. With all 12 keypoints it reproduces them to within a few millimetres; with only hands and feet, the elbows and knees have to be guessed and end up about 8 cm off on average.

Reward: balance first

The first whole-body training run learned to match poses, then fell later in its episodes. Three quarters of its reward was pose accuracy, the balance terms only reacted once a fall had started, and PPO was set to care about roughly the next 2 seconds. A robot on the floor copies nothing, so the reward was rebuilt around balance:

The key new term is the capture point [8]: the robot’s centre of mass plus its horizontal speed divided by √(g / h), where h is the height of the centre of mass. It marks where the robot would have to step to come to a stop. If it lies inside the area covered by the feet on the ground, the robot can recover without stepping; if it lies outside, a fall has already started, even though nothing looks wrong yet. Curriculumfeet-planted motions first; one-leg balancing unlocks at 80 % survival, then stepping

Training runs in two stages: 60 million steps on the motion library, then 40 million more with random pushes on the pelvis (up to 60 N), floor friction varied between 0.6 and 1.3 times normal, and sensor noise, so the policy doesn’t rely on a perfect simulation.

Watching it learn

Every 5 % of a run, the current policy replays the same fixed test episodes for each kind of motion and records a video; every 10 % a backup of the model is saved. The most useful number is survival per motion kind: the fraction of each test episode the robot stays up. Any backup can be turned into a GIF like the one at the top of this page.

Running it

From the training scripts folder of the repository:

# check video recording works (picks EGL automatically over SSH)
python train_pose_tracking.py video

# stage 1, then stage 2 with pushes, friction and noise
python train_pose_tracking.py train
python train_pose_tracking.py train2

# watch the result, or make a GIF of every checkpoint
python train_pose_tracking.py play
python gif_maker.py "logs/pose_stage1/checkpoints/*.zip"

Everything a run produces (training log, test scores, videos and model backups) is written to logs/<run>/.

Results from stage 1

Stage 1 has finished: 60 million steps, which took 10.4 hours with 16 simulated robots. Figure 3 is drawn straight from that run’s training log, progress.csv. The page loads the file and plots it, and the numbers in this section are read from it too.

Feet-planted motions came first. At the start the robot stayed up for 13 % of a test episode. One-leg balance joined training at 1.9 million steps, as soon as both feet-planted kinds reached 80 % survival in training, and by the second test, at 3 million steps, the robot completed every arm-and-waist and whole-body test episode.

Balancing on one leg took longest. Its test score rose from 13 % to 93 %, with big swings on the way. Each test has only two episodes per motion kind, so a single early end moves that kind’s score by up to 50 points.

Stepping was partly learned before it was trained. Stepping motions only joined training at 46.0 million steps, once one-leg balance passed 80 % survival. From 12 million steps on, their test score was already above 70 % in all but one test, probably because lifting a foot and putting it back down is also part of the one-leg motions. From 51 million steps on, the robot completed every stepping test episode.

Falls became rare. In the first training update, 98 % of episodes ended early: 34 % with a fall and the rest by drifting more than 30 cm from the pose. By the end, 4.5 % ended with a fall and 16 % by losing the pose, so most of what is left to fix is accuracy rather than balance. The capture point tells the same story: on average it started −9.6 cm from the edge of the feet, outside them, and finished at +4.6 cm, inside.

Feet are the hardest to place. The pose error over all 12 keypoints fell from 8.8 to 5.6 cm in training. The feet end furthest off, at 9.7 cm against 6.2 cm for the hands, probably because they move furthest in the one-leg and stepping motions.

The last test wasn’t the best. The best test score was 98 %, at 57.0 million steps. The final test dropped to 89 % because whole-body survival fell to 61 %; with two episodes per kind, that can come from a single episode ending early.

Next is stage 2: 40 million more steps with pushes, varied floor friction and sensor noise.
