Rig Construction

To gather tactile data that is repeatable and well labelled, we built a Cartesian test rig that moves a sensor over a sample in a controlled way. The same rig drives every sensor in this work, so differences between datasets come from the sensors and textures rather than from how they were touched. The rig occupies roughly 300 mm by 300 mm by 150 mm and has three linear axes plus a tilt axis.

Hardware

Each axis is driven by a stepper motor:

Table: The rig's axes.
AxisWhat moves
xthe plate holding the sample, back and forth
ythe main body, left and right
zthe sensor, up and down onto the sample
a (tilt)the angle of the plate

A Raspberry Pi Pico runs the motors through two Adafruit stepper motor boards on an I2C bus. Limit switches on the axes let the rig home itself: each axis is driven until its switch is pressed, giving a fixed reference position to count steps from. This also clears any steps a motor may have missed. A force-sensing pad from our tactile sensor work can sit on top of the plate, recording the force on the sample as an extra label gathered automatically during each recording.

Control software

The Pico runs a small listener program (in MicroPython or CircuitPython; an Arduino version also exists) that waits for commands from a PC over a serial connection. On the PC, a Python library sends moves in motor steps, reads the limit switches and reads the force pad. Experiments are short Python scripts: move to a point, lower the sensor, stroke, lift and record. Because each move is an exact number of steps from home, a script can be re-run and the sensor follows the same path again. The code is open so others can build the rig and gather their own datasets.

Data collection

This rig was used to gather texture data by stroking the sensor along 1.5mm straight lines in 100 different directions along evenly spaced radii of a semicircle centred on the original touching point. The sensor moved across fixed-down samples from the texture set. Recordings were gathered at various contact forces, using touching point forces of 0.0785 – 0.0824N, 1.9031 – 1.9228N, 3.0039 – 3.0235N and 4.336 – 4.367N. The dataset consists of 3000 items gathered over 15 textures. Each dataset item contains multiple sensor readings concatenated over the time that the sensor is in contact with the surface (approximately 10 seconds).

Moving the sensor across varying vectors shows the sensor's ability to classify the different textures with linear stroking movements, this is the most common way of gathering textural datasets. We also investigated how the sensor-classifier pairs performed on sensations they were not trained on. To do this, we collected a non-linear movement dataset by moving the sensor in circular movements of increasing radii (1cm, 1.75cm, 2.5cm) while increasing the contact force by approximately 17 grams on each iteration. We refer to this as the non-linear dataset.

Simulation and sim-to-real (ongoing work)

The rig now has a simulated twin in TacTip-gym, our MuJoCo simulator of a soft-bodied TacTip. The aim is to learn how what the real TacTip senses relates to what the simulated one senses, in both directions. Then models trained in simulation can be given what the real sensor would see, and real readings can be turned into simulated ones so a policy trained in simulation can run on the real sensor.

The simulated rig

The simulated TacTip is a soft skin made of linked nodes with markers on its inside, mounted on the same three axes as the real rig, above the same 3D-printed texture blocks (29.3 × 30 × 7 mm). The simulator gives several readings of the sensor, any of which can be chosen as an input in the gym:

ReadingWhat it is

Depth imageHow far the inside of the skin is from a camera in the tip, as the real depth camera measures it
Real TacTip (learned)The simulated reading passed through a learned mapping, giving what the real sensor would show

Calibrating the real rig to the simulated one

Both rigs start from a reset position: the simulated sensor hovers 10 mm above the centre of the block, and the real rig homes on its limit switches. Each real axis's steps per millimetre is measured by driving it a known number of steps from its limit switch and measuring how far it went. We then drive both rigs with the keyboard, side by side, to matching poses over the block. From these pairs the software works out where the block sits in motor steps, and flags any pose that disagrees with the others. Once calibrated, moving the sensor in the simulator moves the real sensor to the same place.

Paired datasets

With the rigs matched, the same motion runs on both, so every real sample has a simulated twin. The motions are presses, taps, straight and depth-changing strokes, circles, spirals, figure-of-eights, zigzags and smooth random paths. Each is cut into points half a millimetre apart; at each point both rigs move there, settle, and record. The real sensor is a TacTip with an Intel RealSense D405 inside it: its colour stream is the TacTip image and its depth stream measures the skin. The session used below has 18,320 paired samples over 320 trajectories and 16 textures. Each sample holds the simulated markers and camera image, the real TacTip image, and the real depth image.

Right: the real depth image; blank areas have no reading, because the camera sits closer to the skin than it can measure.

Learning the mapping

Whole images. Our first approach mapped whole images. One autoencoder compresses the simulated image to a short code, another compresses the real image, and small networks translate between the codes. The simulated autoencoder reproduced its images well (error 0.10, where 1 is no better than always predicting the average image and 0 is perfect). The translation learned nothing: 1.00 from simulated to real, 0.85 back. One reason is that the real camera drifts. Its brightness wanders by several grey levels between trajectories while nothing touches the sensor, and the simulator cannot predict that.

Figure: Mean brightness of the real image before contact, for every fourth trajectory. It varies between about 125 and 136 grey levels (standard deviation 2.5) with nothing touching the sensor.
Do the real images follow the simulated ones? To catch problems like this before training, a pairing check measures, sample by sample, how much the real image has changed since before contact, and how much the simulated one has. If both rigs made the same contact, the two should rise and fall together. In the raw images they barely did (correlation 0.31), and contact changed the real image by only 1.4 times the camera's own noise. Comparing every real image with the same trajectory's frames from before contact removes the drift. Converting images to their edges with a Sobel filter, as in our texture classification work, nearly doubled the correlation, to 0.58.

Figure: How much each image changes from before contact (Sobel edges), along four trajectories. Orange is the real image, blue is the simulated one, and dashed is the commanded depth (right axis). The real image changes strongly while the simulated one barely moves: the simulated sensor was hardly touching.
Pins instead of pixels. Our current approach works with the markers. The software finds the 132 white pins in every real frame and follows each one through its trajectory. It finds them about 97% of the time. Each pin's position comes from the colour image. Its depth comes from the depth image where there is a reading, about half the time. Movement is measured from the trajectory's frames before contact, so the drift drops out.

Figure: Left: the 132 pins found in the real image at the deepest point of a press. Right: how far each pin moved from before contact (arrows, enlarged); colour is how far it moved towards the camera, in mm, where the depth image has a reading.

Figure: Along one press: the real pins' mean sideways shift (px) and push towards the camera (mm), against the simulated markers' shift (mm) in the original recording. The real pins move clearly; the simulated markers hardly move at all.
Correcting the height. The plots above show that, as recorded, the simulated sensor was pressing much less than the real one. So we re-ran every recorded trajectory in simulation, with the same texture, path and points, pressing the simulated sensor deeper by a range of offsets. For each offset we compared the depth at which the simulated markers start moving with the depth at which the real image starts changing. The two line up at a 2.5 mm offset, and the pairs correlate best at 1.5 mm. Either way, the calibrated height was out by 1.5 to 2.5 mm. The re-simulated session uses 2.5 mm.

Figure: Choosing how much deeper the simulated sensor should press. Left: the depth at which the real image starts changing, minus the depth at which the simulated markers start moving (0 = they touch at the same depth). Right: how closely the two change together.
The marker map. On the re-simulated session, a small network maps the 71 simulated markers' movement to the 132 real pins' movement, and back. It was tested on whole motions held out from training.

Where this leaves us. The simulated-to-real direction works: from the simulated sensor alone, the map predicts the real pins' pattern and size of movement, explaining about 58% of their variation. The real-to-simulated direction is weaker, at about a quarter. That direction is the one needed to run a policy trained in simulation on the real sensor. The networks stopped improving within a few epochs, so the limit is how well the recorded pairs match, not the model. The next step is to record a new session with the height error corrected in the calibration, rather than after the fact. In TacTip-gym, the trained map is already an input, so a policy trained in simulation can be given what the real sensor would see.

Dataset

Perhaps understandably, most papers on tactile sensing for texture classification have used textured materials that are easily accessible, and to date no standard texture set has been created. Past texture sets are often of different sizes which hampers comparability because, as shown in section, some texture classifiers perform well when distinguishing between small numbers of textures but degrade sharply as the number of classes increase. As established current datasets tend to use items that appear in the labs of the researchers. Often these include some form of carpet, hard materials and fabrics. Our texture dataset was designed to try and include commonalities between previous sets and to represent a range of material properties that a walking robot may come into contact with, such as indoors or flat outdoors surfaces. These included soft/hard bodies, coarse/smooth surfaces and surfaces with raised aspects.

Friction Prediction

Initially, we used a range of regression models (Linear, Ridge, Logistic) but found that the Random Forest classifier significantly outperformed the other regression models. Therefore, we only proceeded with the results of Ridge and Random Forest, which performed better than the Linear and Logistic models.

Friction detection model performance was calculated using the mean squared error (MSE) between predicted and true values, with the mean calculated over all textures in the dataset. Although there is more noise in the voltage readings of the electrical sensors, the Random Forest regression model handled this well.

Table 1 displays the results, showing a smaller error from the optical sensor. However, the relative performance of the electrical sensors is good and better than for the texture classification task. The TacTip values are more closely clustered around the line of best fit. Although the electrical sensor readings are noisier and more widely spread, their regression model produced a close match to the actual values. The results shown in Table 2 are for the clear-silicone TacTip and for various configurations of the electrical sensor (which also has a silicone tip).

The Random Forest Regression classifier was found to be one of the quickest to train and highest performing for friction prediction across both sensors. We performed a comparison to evaluate how the different classifiers performed for the task of friction prediction using mean squared error as the metric. The same models were compared for both the electrical and optical sensors, where appropriate for the data type.

The LSTM and CNN classifiers both used hidden layers of 350 nodes. The neural models had one output node and were trained with the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. The results are shown in Table 1. The Random Forest was the highest performing model.

Morphology Experiments


Each image was 110 X 120 pixels over 20 frames, till we cut this down to 4 frames each after hyper-parameter sweeps to find the minimum length to still maintain accuracy. The video shows movement of the TacTip sensor over various directions along 1.5 mm straight lines in 100 different directions. Each direction had evenly spaced radii of a semicircle centred on the original touching point. The rig used three-axis of stepper motors of dimensions 300mm by 300mm by 150mm to allow direction and force to be applied to the sensor across each surface. The forces used were of 0.0785 – 0.0824N, 1.9031 – 1.9228N, 3.0039 – 3.0235N and 4.336 – 4.367N pushed directly onto the texture with the sensor facing downwards so the tip of the soft body would be in contact first.
The new marker skins were attached to the rig setup and used to gather the same dataset as previous experiments. We apply Sobel filters and reduce the images by mean scaling. All the preprocessing steps and model parameters matched that of all previous experiments}. We chose the CNN model due to its success in previous experiments. We used molds with holes, groves and smooth to make a range of tips. Then we coloured in patterns with white paint.

The results of the moolds can be seen on the TacTip design page. We used a Convolutional neural network (CNN) we adopted a network with hidden layer of 128 nodes, along with 15 output nodes (one for each possible texture class). The CNN was more interpretable from an image point of view making it the ideal choice, though other models have been used on the same classification task. We used activation functions of ReLU on each layer, kernel size 3 and a stride of 1. Cross entropy loss was used with the stochastic gradient descent optimizer at learning rate 0.005. Images were converted into a long image representing the temporality of the video. After experiments, we could get a high accuracy at as low as 4 frames from the video. Each morphology of TacTip marker design was trained over 20 trials and accuracy averaged in the results. We used Saliancy maps to analyse what was going on inside the CNNs to help distinguish useful features. Saliency maps are a technique for visualizing which parts of an input most influence a model’s prediction, effectively highlighting the pixels where small changes would cause the largest change in the output. In our implementation, we first prepared and normalised the input images, enabled gradient tracking, and ran them through the trained model to obtain the predicted class. We then performed a backward pass on the output corresponding to that predicted class, which gave us the gradient of the class score with respect to each input pixel. Taking the absolute value of these gradients produced a saliency tensor showing pixel-level importance. Finally, we collapsed the channels, visualized the resulting 2D saliency map with a heat-map, revealing which parts of the tactile image the model relied on most strongly for its decision.



Despite having no markers, the no marker design performed at a much higher accuracy than the alternative morphologies. All the designs had high accuracy. When we looked at the sobel-filtered images we noticed there were glare marks on the surface of the gel. These LED markers moved differently dependent on the force on the sensor. These were clearly standing in for the absence of markers. In summary of the morphology experiments, the size of optical markers can indeed impact how the accuracy performs at lower resolutions. This was only evaluated on our task for texture classification, however more work in the area of object and edge recognition would further enhance the study on how pattern shape could help reduce the resolution of tactile images. This work can help decrease the computational costs of this technology. The novel work in morphology changes for classification could be enhanced by using more procedurally generated morphology to explore a wider range of designs. Our focus has been texture classification, rather than edge detection, though experimentation into more than just texture classification could reveal more about the morphologies pros and cons. The methods discussed such as saliency maps would be very useful to determine which aspects of the morphology are useful for each task.
